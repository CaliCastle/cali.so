I decided to stop buying SaaS.

We still pay for plenty of software. I am not going to rebuild payments, accounting, cloud infrastructure, or every mature service we use just to save a few dollars in monthly subscriptions.

Software still needs maintenance after it has been built. Internal tools accumulate technical debt just like customer-facing products do. Rebuilding everything to satisfy a handful of personal preferences is not particularly AI-native. It usually means someone has underestimated the long-term cost.

But over the past few months, I have stopped searching for the tool that comes closest to what I need.

**Sometimes, when the right tool does not exist, it is easier to build one.**

This did not begin at the company.

After our second child was born, I wanted an app for tracking diapers, breastfeeding sessions, and bottle feeds. There were plenty of options, and I downloaded almost every comparable app I could find. But after using them repeatedly throughout the day, I kept running into details that bothered me.

Some had too many features, turning the home screen into a control panel. Some buried frequent actions several levels deep, which made them difficult to use while holding a baby in one arm. Some looked cute but were too slow in practice. Others kept accumulating features until even the most basic records became cumbersome.

Most of the Chinese apps I tried were filled with advertising, including the familiar full-screen launch ad. International alternatives were usually cleaner, but their visual design rarely met my expectations. Both sides had their own problems, and none of them quite scratched the itch.

**I could not find an app that matched what I wanted, so I thought: why not build one myself?**

AI moved the business logic, tests, and early prototypes forward quickly. The real work was turning something that could run into something I would willingly use every day.

That polishing process took two months.

At the time, I did not think of it as part of a broader shift. It was a deeply personal need. The right product did not exist, I had the ability to build it, and I wanted to make something for my own family.

Then the same thing started happening inside the company.

## Software That Was Not Worth Building Before Is Worth Building Now

Zolplay hires people in China and around the world.

We tried several applicant tracking systems. Most of them looked complete in a product demo, but once we placed them inside our actual hiring process, there was always something we had to work around.

How should candidate emails enter the hiring pipeline automatically? How should interviews be scheduled across regions and time zones? How should candidates be organized and evaluated against the criteria for each role? How should interviewers submit independent feedback before the team decides whether to move someone forward?

Every product solved part of the process. None of them had grown around the way we actually worked.

In the past, I probably would have accepted that mismatch.

Building a custom ATS would have required product, design, and engineering time. It would have taken at least two months, possibly three. Internal tools rarely win when they have to compete with client work and company products for attention. We would have bought the closest available SaaS product, then asked the team to fill the remaining gaps by hand.

**This time, I simply started building.**

Over one weekend, <InlineProductName product="Codex" />, <InlineProductName product="Claude Code" />, and I assembled the complete workflow: automatic email ingestion, organizing and scoring candidates against role-specific criteria, interview scheduling, and the team's post-interview evaluation process.

It is not a prototype that exists only in screenshots. It is managing real candidates today. The final judgment still belongs to the team, and the system is working well.

**→ What changed was not just development speed. What changed was which software deserved to enter the roadmap at all.**

An internal tool that would never have been prioritized before could now be tested over a weekend.

The build-versus-buy discussion used to be fairly straightforward.

Buying software was cheap. Building software was expensive. Unless your needs were unusually specific, buying was almost always the rational choice.

AI has not removed maintenance costs, and it has not made every internal build a good idea. But it has clearly moved the boundary. Building software around the way your team works no longer has to mean starting a long, expensive development project.

So we built **Control**.

It is Zolplay's own customer relationship management system. It manages customers, potential clients, project opportunities, and follow-up activity.

Then we built **Dex**.

We increasingly felt that Notion had become too heavy. It kept adding AI and database capabilities, but not necessarily focusing more deeply on the things we valued most: clean documents, a knowledge base, Markdown, project handoffs, client assets, and content structures that agents could reliably read and use.

So we built our own document and knowledge system. Today, the team uses Dex to share internal documents, project handoffs, and client assets. We also use it to discuss ideas directly with AI agents.

Seen separately, these look like a few unrelated products.

- <InlineProductName product="ATS" /> manages candidates

But as they appeared one after another, I realized we were not simply recreating a few SaaS products.

**→ We were moving different parts of the company into software we controlled.**

## Stop Making the Company Fit the Software

Most companies buy general-purpose software and then reshape their work around it.

When the fields do not fit, they add another table. When two workflows do not connect, someone moves the information manually. When the permissions are insufficient, they install a plugin. When context is scattered across several systems, meetings and Slack messages temporarily stitch everything back together.

**The cost of SaaS has never been limited to the subscription.**

It also includes how much of a team's natural way of working must gradually be abandoned to accommodate the software.

That does not mean everything should be built internally.

Payments, tax, compliance, cloud infrastructure, and mature general-purpose capabilities should usually be maintained by teams that specialize in them. The fact that AI can write code does not mean a design and software company should casually rebuild Stripe, GitHub, or its accounting system.

**A different category of software is worth reconsidering.**

How are hiring standards applied? How are potential clients recognized? How is project experience preserved? Which decisions require review, and what kind of review? These questions may appear to be software features, but they are also part of how a company works.

Previously, we could only store these workflows inside general-purpose products built by someone else.

For a team like ours, adapting the software to the company has now become a realistic option.

The Zolplay website we are currently rebuilding will continue in the same direction.

The new site will not be limited to presenting our work and services.

We plan to let visitors speak directly with an AI agent on the website. They will be able to understand what Zolplay offers, the approximate budget range for a project, and whether there appears to be a good fit. The agent will organize the initial information and send suitable leads to Slack, **where I review them**, before they are recorded in Control and enter the appropriate follow-up process.

```mermaid caption="The agent organizes and qualifies. The team keeps the real decision."

  V["Visitor"] --> W["Website agent"] --> U["Services, pricing, and requirements"] --> Q["Initial qualification"] --> H{"Internal review<br/>Human judgment"} --> C["Control CRM"] --> F["Follow-up"]
```

The website receives the opportunity. The agent organizes it. <InlineProductName product="Slack" /> brings a person into the decision. Control preserves the relationship and its status.

**At this point, software is no longer just a tool in an employee's hands. It has become an essential part of the company itself.**

But as development becomes cheaper, a new bottleneck becomes more visible.

**→ The difficult part is no longer only writing the software. It is deciding which ways of working deserve to be encoded in software.**

## Code Is Getting Cheaper. Judgment Is Getting More Expensive.

One increasingly popular idea about AI-native talent is:

> **Learning Rate &gt; Experience.**

**I do not completely agree.**

Learning speed obviously matters. Models, tools, and workflows keep changing. Anyone who relies only on old answers will fall behind quickly.

But when building real products, experience often determines whether the details succeed or fail.

Someone can learn SwiftUI quickly. They can ask an agent to produce the business logic, unit tests, and interface tests. That does not mean they will understand why a product that already runs still does not feel right.

In the baby companion app I am preparing to release, diaper tracking, breastfeeding timers, and bottle-feed records are among the most frequent actions.

In the first version, I placed them in large cards across the home screen. Two cards per row meant that only a few tools occupied most of the screen.

I later changed the layout to three columns. The information density improved, but the touch targets became narrower and easier to trigger accidentally.

Then I moved the tools into a quick-action menu. Initially, the entry point was on the right side of the tab bar, and some users asked for it to be moved to the left because they often operated the app with their left hand. But with the SwiftUI and system tab bar approach I was using, I could not customize the layout that freely. Fully satisfying that request would have required building a custom tab bar, which would also mean giving up many of the benefits of the native system behavior and Liquid Glass effects.

Every option was technically possible. Every option had a reasonable argument behind it.

AI could implement each one quickly. It could not decide which compromise I should accept.

In the end, I returned to the native tab bar and placed the quick-action entry point in the center. I also replaced the fixed list of features with a tool collection similar to the <InlineProductName product="App Store" />, allowing each family to add or remove the tools they needed.

The result supported both left- and right-handed use, preserved the native system behavior, and prevented the product from becoming increasingly bloated as more features were added.

**Having seen a lot is not the same as having collected a lot of beautiful screenshots.**

It means knowing when a small detail genuinely makes something easier to use. It means remembering the feeling of using a product and suddenly realizing that someone cared enough to get a particular interaction right.

I want people to feel the same care in the products I make.

## Let the System Inherit Experience. Keep the Decisions with People.

**Experience has not become less valuable because of AI.**

It is beginning to change its form.

```mermaid caption="People curate experience before it enters a system that can run repeatedly."
flowchart TB
  E1["Failure · comparison · long-term use"] --> H{"Human selection<br/>and curation"}
  E2["User feedback · project tradeoffs"] --> H
  H --> K["Principles · counterexamples · review standards<br/>Boundaries · examples"]
  K --> S["Prompts · workflows · memory<br/>Skills · agents · internal software"]
  S --> R["The company's everyday capability"]
```

In the past, experience primarily existed inside an individual's head. Someone who had built products for ten years knew which problems deserved attention and which clever-looking ideas would eventually become burdens. But the team could rarely inherit that judgment in full.

Now, part of that experience can be selected, organized, and turned into prompts, workflows, memory, review standards, examples, counterexamples, and even skills that agents can call.

The next person may not need to spend another ten years stepping into every one of the same holes.

But there is a condition that cannot be skipped.

**Someone must first know which experience is worth preserving.**

Without sufficiently deep practice, it is difficult to distinguish a real principle from a lucky outcome. It is also difficult to know which details made a product work and which were merely personal habits.

AI can shorten the time required to distribute experience. It cannot create experience out of nothing.

The area I am currently least willing to hand over entirely to agents is visual design.

![A row of candidate options passes a review boundary into larger decision orbits](./figure-7-system-boundary.jpg#1600x900 "A system can generate options. A person still decides what is worth shipping.")

That is not because AI cannot create an attractive image or interface. It can already generate many directions quickly and repeatedly move closer to a specified style.

The problem is that visual design exposes the boundary of the system more clearly than almost anything else.

**A solution can run, pass its tests, and even perform reasonably well in the data, yet still not deserve to exist in that particular form.**

A product should be beautiful, useful, durable, and occasionally delightful without letting its motion steal attention from the work. These qualities can be described. They can gradually be encoded into a design system, examples, and review standards. But they are difficult to reduce to a complete formula.

AI can keep moving closer to the shape we want.

**Someone still has to decide whether this is something we are willing to ship.**

Data can help us evaluate whether a product is effective, but it cannot decide what the company should pursue. Code can verify whether something runs, but it cannot determine whether it deserves to be built.

Repeatable work can move to agents. Experience can be preserved inside systems. Human attention can return to judgment, taste, and decisions that cannot be measured only by whether the task was completed.

I increasingly believe that being AI-native is not a new label for talent.

It is, first of all, a redistribution.

- **Experience** moves from individual memory into systems that can run
- **Repeatable work** moves from people to agents
- **Human time** returns to the places that still require choice and responsibility

We will continue buying plenty of SaaS. Mature, general-purpose capabilities should be maintained by the teams best equipped to maintain them.

But the parts that carry how Zolplay hires, understands clients, preserves knowledge, and makes tradeoffs are no longer things we want to leave entirely inside software designed by someone else.

What we decided to stop buying is not SaaS.

**It is software that asks us to surrender the way we work before it can fit.**
